INTRODUCTION:

The scapula, commonly known as the shoulder blade, is a large, flat, triangular bone that forms an essential component of the pectoral (shoulder) girdle. It is located on the posterolateral aspect of the thoracic cage, extending roughly from the second to the seventh rib. The scapula connects the upper limb to the axial skeleton indirectly through the clavicle and plays a crucial role in mobility, stability, and functional efficiency of the upper limb.
Anatomically, the scapula has two surfaces (costal and dorsal), three borders (superior, medial/vertebral, and lateral/axillary), and three angles (superior, inferior, and lateral). The costal surface is concave and forms the subscapular fossa, which accommodates the subscapularis muscle. The dorsal surface is divided by a prominent ridge called the spine of the scapula into the supraspinous and infraspinous fossae, providing attachment to muscles involved in arm movement. The lateral angle bears the glenoid cavity, which articulates with the head of the humerus to form the glenohumeral joint, one of the most mobile joints in the human body.
From an anthropological perspective, the scapula is of great importance because it exhibits sexual dimorphism, population variation, and functional adaptation. Male scapulae are generally larger, thicker, and more robust, while female scapulae are relatively smaller and lighter. Variations in scapular dimensions and shape have been used to study racial characteristics, evolutionary trends, and habitual activity patterns. The morphology of the scapula reflects muscular development and biomechanical stress, which are influenced by occupation, lifestyle, and cultural practices.
In physical and forensic anthropology, scapular measurements are used for sex determination, reconstruction of body build, and identification of skeletal remains, especially when long bones are fragmented or unavailable. In comparative and evolutionary studies, the scapula provides valuable information about changes in locomotion, posture, and upper limb function, particularly in relation to arboreal versus terrestrial adaptations in primates and early humans.
Thus, the scapula is not merely a flat bone of anatomical interest but a key skeletal element that offers insights into biological variation, functional anatomy, evolutionary history, and forensic identification.
